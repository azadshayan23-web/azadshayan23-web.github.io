Project Overview

Improving crack response in cementitious materials

Cracking can reduce the durability of cementitious materials by allowing water and aggressive agents to enter. The study evaluated bacterial self-healing mortar as a potential way to limit crack-related deterioration.

The research investigated how calcium lactate dosage and healing temperature influenced apparent surface crack closure and compressive-strength recovery in Bacillus subtilis mortar. It also developed preliminary machine-learning models within the measured experimental range.

The project was completed by a four-member undergraduate team. This page presents the group project and does not assign unsupported individual responsibility for specific experiments or results.

Objectives

Research objectives

01

Prepare bacterial mortar

Use Bacillus subtilis at 10⁸ cells/mL, a cement:sand ratio of 1:2, and a water-cement ratio of 0.45.

02

Evaluate nutrient dosage

Investigate calcium lactate concentrations of 0.5%, 1.5%, and 10% by cement mass.

03

Evaluate temperature

Compare healing conditions at -15 °C, 20 °C, 40 °C, and 50 °C.

04

Measure mechanical response

Determine compressive strength before crack induction and after the healing period.

05

Assess crack healing

Calculate healing efficiency and identify the best-performing tested dosage-temperature condition.

06

Develop preliminary models

Use XGBoost to predict Day-28 healing efficiency and post-healing compressive strength within the tested range.

Materials & Methodology

Verified experimental parameters

Binder & aggregate
Ordinary Portland cement and natural sand passing the No. 16 sieve (1.18 mm) and retained on the No. 30 sieve (600 µm)
Base mix
Cement:sand = 1:2 by mass; water-cement ratio = 0.45
Specimen
50 mm × 50 mm × 50 mm mortar cubes
Bacteria
Bacillus subtilis suspension at 10⁸ cells/mL
Suspension
10% replacement of total mixing water by mass in bacterial batches
Nutrient
Calcium lactate at 0.5%, 1.5%, and 10% by cement mass
Initial curing
24 hours in molds, followed by 28 days of water curing at approximately 23–27 °C
Crack induction
1 mm steel wire beneath each cube; load applied at 1.5 kN/s and stopped at the first visible surface crack
Healing exposure
28 days at -15 °C, 20 °C, 40 °C, or 50 °C at approximately 95% relative humidity
Crack monitoring
ImageJ measurement at a fixed crack location on Days 0, 7, 14, 21, and 28

Control and bacterial mortar groups

The control contained neither bacteria nor calcium lactate. Batches A, B, and C used the same target bacterial concentration with different calcium lactate dosages.

Machine Learning

Preliminary XGBoost models

Two regression models used temperature, calcium lactate dosage, and Day-0 crack width as inputs. The targets were Day-28 healing efficiency and post-healing compressive strength.

  • Healing-efficiency dataset: 8 independent batch-temperature conditions
  • Strength dataset: 24 specimen results in 8 unique predictor conditions
  • Validation: Leave-One-Out for healing efficiency and Leave-One-Condition-Out for strength
  • Measured prediction domain: -15 °C to 50 °C, 0.5% to 1.5% calcium lactate, and 0.279 to 0.722 mm Day-0 crack width
1.00 ppHealing-efficiency cross-validated MAE
1.71 ppHealing-efficiency RMSE
2.18 MPaStrength-model grouped cross-validated MAE
2.57 MPaStrength-model RMSE
The models are a small-dataset proof of concept. They are not externally validated and are not intended for structural design, safety assessment, or extrapolation beyond the measured experimental domain.

Discussion

Engineering interpretation and limitations

Dosage response was not linear

Batch C (1.5%) produced the highest pre-crack mean strength, while Batch A (10%) produced the lowest. Batch A also used granular calcium lactate, so dosage and physical form were not fully independent.

Temperature influenced healing

The -15 °C condition showed approximately 50% closure after 28 days. The tested 20 °C, 40 °C, and 50 °C representative points reached complete apparent closure by Day 28.

Surface closure was not equal to full recovery

Complete apparent surface closure at the measured point did not produce identical compressive-strength recovery at 20 °C, 40 °C, and 50 °C.

The optimum is experiment-specific

Batch C at 40 °C was the best-performing condition within the tested program. It is not presented as a universal mix-design recommendation.

SEM, XRD, permeability testing, long-term field exposure, and full-scale structural testing were not part of this experiment. “Apparent surface closure” is therefore used instead of claiming confirmed internal crack filling or mineral composition.
